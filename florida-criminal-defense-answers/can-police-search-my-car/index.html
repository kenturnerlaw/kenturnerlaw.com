Can Police Search My Car in Florida?

Sometimes, but not merely because an officer asks. A vehicle search may be based on a warrant, valid consent, probable cause, or another recognized exception. A person may calmly state that he or she does not consent and should not physically interfere.

Consent

Consent is one of the most common asserted grounds for a search. Consent must be voluntary and the search generally must remain within the scope of the permission given. A refusal to consent does not authorize resistance; it preserves the issue for later review.

Probable cause and the automobile exception

Under the automobile exception, officers may search areas of a vehicle where the object of the search could reasonably be located when they have probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence or contraband. The claimed facts supporting probable cause matter, including observations, statements, odors, alerts, video, and the reliability of the information used.

Search incident to arrest and inventory searches

An arrest does not automatically permit an unlimited vehicle search. Different rules govern searches incident to arrest, protective searches, and inventory searches following lawful impoundment. Courts examine the actual justification, scope, and procedures used.

What to do during the encounter

Keep your hands visible, comply with lawful commands, do not argue the case roadside, and do not obstruct the search. State clearly that you do not consent. Preserve dash-camera or phone video, towing records, property receipts, and other evidence for counsel.

Primary legal framework

Vehicle searches are governed principally by the Fourth Amendment, article I, section 12 of the Florida Constitution, Florida statutes, and controlling appellate decisions. Whether evidence may be suppressed depends on the facts proved at a hearing.
