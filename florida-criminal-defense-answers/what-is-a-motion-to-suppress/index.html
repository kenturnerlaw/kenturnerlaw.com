What Is a Motion to Suppress in Florida?

Quick answer

A motion to suppress asks a court to prevent the State from using evidence or statements obtained in violation of constitutional, statutory, or procedural protections. It does not automatically dismiss a case, but excluding important evidence can substantially change the prosecution.

What evidence may be challenged?

A suppression motion may concern physical evidence, drugs, weapons, digital data, statements, identifications, test results, or evidence discovered after an allegedly unlawful stop, detention, search, seizure, or interrogation.

The motion must identify the legal and factual grounds

The defense ordinarily identifies the challenged evidence, explains why the government’s conduct was unlawful, and requests an evidentiary hearing when material facts are disputed. Reports, body-camera video, dispatch records, warrants, affidavits, photographs, and witness testimony may be important.

What happens at the hearing?

The court hears testimony and argument, resolves factual disputes, and decides whether the evidence may be used. The burden of proof can depend on the type of search or seizure and whether the government relies on a warrant, consent, probable cause, or another exception.

What happens if the motion is granted?

The excluded evidence cannot be used in the manner covered by the court’s ruling. The prosecution may continue with other admissible evidence, reduce or dismiss charges, or seek review where permitted. The practical effect depends on how central the suppressed evidence was to the case.
