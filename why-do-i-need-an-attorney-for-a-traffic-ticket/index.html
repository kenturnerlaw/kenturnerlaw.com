Florida traffic-ticket representation

Why Do I Need an Attorney for a Traffic Ticket?

Quick answer: Because Florida treats payment as a conviction—and the real cost is points, insurance, and license risk, not the fine printed on the citation.

Speeding alone accounts for nearly one million Florida tickets in recent years. Most non-criminal moving violations carry 3 to 4 points. Hit 12 points in 12 months and your license can be suspended. An attorney exists to stop you from buying that outcome when a better path is still open.

Why an Attorney Matters

Most people look at a $150–$300 fine and decide a lawyer is overkill. That is the trap. Paying admits guilt. Points go on your record. Insurance can jump 20% or more for years. Criminal traffic charges—suspended license, DUI—do not offer traffic school as an escape hatch.

Florida has more than 17 million licensed drivers, heavy tourist traffic, and aggressive enforcement—from I-95 in Miami to school zones statewide. The stats below are the reason counsel is often the cheaper move: what the ticket costs, what points do, and where drivers get hit hardest.

In many routine cases we appear for you, seek dismissal or reduction, and protect the record when the law allows. Flat-fee traffic representation often starts at $100. Related: Don't Pay Your Traffic Ticket Yet · Negative Consequences of Paying · What Do I Do When I Get a Traffic Ticket?

How Florida's Point System Works

A moving-violation conviction adds points that generally stay on your record for three years. Stack them too fast and the state can suspend your license:

PointsWindowConsequence
1212 months30-day suspension
1818 months3-month suspension
2436 months1-year suspension

For many non-criminal citations, electing a Basic Driver Improvement (BDI) course within 30 days may keep points from being assessed. Criminal charges, CDL restrictions, and some serious speeding cases are different—and simply paying online is still a conviction. See why paying can cost more than the fine.

What a Traffic Attorney Can Do

  • Appear in court so you often do not have to miss work
  • Pursue dismissal, reduction, or adjudication withheld when available
  • Protect against unnecessary points and insurance damage
  • Handle criminal traffic charges (suspended license, DUI) that school cannot fix
  • Beat the 30-day clock with a plan instead of a panic payment

No attorney can promise a result. The point of hiring one is to pursue the best available outcome before you give those options away.

Miami Traffic Tickets: What Drivers See Most

In Miami and Miami-Dade, the most common traffic citation is speeding—including automated school-zone speed camera violations and traditional unlawful-speed tickets. Other frequent moving violations include running red lights, improper lane changes, failure to yield, and illegal turns. Documentation problems (expired tags, registration, proof of insurance) and criminal traffic offenses—especially driving with a suspended license and DUI—are also common.

Local reporting bears that out. According to the Miami Herald (community Miami-Dade coverage citing Clerk and Comptroller data), school-zone speed cameras accounted for about 68% of Miami-Dade speeding tickets in early 2026—tens of thousands of camera citations in a single quarter, far outpacing officer-issued speeding tickets.

If your Miami citation came from a school-zone camera, a red-light camera, or a roadside stop, do not treat the mail notice like a parking fine. An attorney can tell you whether to contest, elect school, or appear—before the 30-day window closes.

#1 Speeding

Florida's #1 citation. Enforcement is heavy in urban corridors and rural highways; Duval County (Jacksonville) often leads per capita, with high volumes in Miami-Dade, Broward, and Hillsborough as well.

School-zone and construction-zone fines can be doubled. At 30 mph or more over the limit, you generally cannot elect traffic school and must appear in court. Without counsel or another lawful resolution, 3–4 points can drive insurance increases of 20% or more for years—turning a $150 ticket into a multi-thousand-dollar problem. That gap is why you hire an attorney.

#2 Running a Red Light

Camera programs and officer stops generate large citation volumes. Typical exposure: 4 points, with camera citations often starting around $158 and officer-issued tickets higher. In Florida, a red-light camera citation is still a Uniform Traffic Citation—it can hit your record if you simply pay it. Yellow timing at high-speed approaches frustrates many drivers; contesting may require a hearing, and the outcome is never guaranteed.

#3 Driving With a Suspended or Revoked License

Florida's most common criminal traffic offense—on the order of nearly 100,000 citations a year. Many drivers learn about a suspension only after another stop. Triggers include unpaid tickets, no insurance, failure to appear, and point accumulation.

A first offense is typically a second-degree misdemeanor: jail exposure up to 60 days, additional suspension, fines up to $500, and possible impoundment. Repeat offenses escalate (including felony exposure). Not eligible for traffic school. Check your status on the FLHSMV site before you drive—and get counsel if charged.

#4 Improper Lane Change

3 points; fines vary by county (often roughly $129–$179). Covers unsafe merges, cutting off other drivers, and failing to signal (Florida generally requires signaling at least 100 feet before a lane change). Common on congested interstates.

#5 Failure to Yield Right-of-Way

3 points. Especially common at intersections and pedestrian crossings. Florida ranks among the most dangerous states for pedestrians, and agencies prioritize right-of-way enforcement.

#6 Texting While Driving

A primary offense in Florida—officers can stop you for it alone. Rough guide:

  • First offense: about $30+; often no points (school/work zones differ)
  • Second within 5 years: about $60+ and 3 points
  • School/work zones: higher stakes; crash while texting can mean 6 points and doubled fines

Manual texting, email, and messaging while moving are covered; voice-activated use is generally treated differently. Some counties allow a wireless-device safety course on a first offense.

#7 Following Too Closely (Tailgating)

3 points. Subjective—there is no fixed foot-distance in the statute. Florida's sudden rain makes short following distances especially dangerous. Usually a non-criminal moving violation.

#8 Passing a Stopped School Bus

4 points; fines often start around $265+. Camera enforcement has expanded. On roads without a raised median, traffic in both directions generally must stop for a school bus stop signal. A painted center line is not a raised median.

#9 DUI

Roughly 44,000 DUI arrests a year statewide. Criminal, with mandatory minimums that escalate. First-offense exposure commonly includes fines of $500–$1,000, jail up to 6 months, license revocation periods, community service, DUI school, and possible ignition interlock. BAC limit is generally 0.08% (21+); under-21 Zero Tolerance is much lower. A first breath-test refusal can trigger automatic suspension even before a conviction. Not eligible for traffic school. Call a lawyer immediately.

#10 Move Over Law

Florida's Move Over law now covers more than emergency vehicles—it can include vehicles stopped on the roadside with hazards, flares, or emergency signage. Move over a lane if safe; if you cannot, slow at least 20 mph below the posted limit. Violations often carry a $500+ fine and 3 points. State reporting has cited tens of thousands of Move Over citations a year and hundreds of related crashes. Usually non-criminal and often school-eligible—worth protecting given the fine size.

What Slows Resolution Down

  • Missing the 30-day election deadline with the clerk
  • Holding a CDL (BDI rules differ)
  • Already using a recent BDI election (often once per 12 months; ask the clerk about alternatives)
  • Assuming a criminal charge or 30+ mph over is “just a ticket”
  • Ignoring the citation entirely—default guilt, fees, and suspension risk

Common Questions

Why do I need an attorney for a traffic ticket?

So you do not confuse a cheap fine with a cheap outcome. Payment is a conviction. Points and insurance usually cost more than the citation—and more than many flat attorney fees.

What is my traffic ticket going to cost if I just pay?

Fine + court costs + points + insurance for years. Speeding alone drives a huge share of Florida's nearly one million recent citations. That is the stack an attorney is hired to interrupt.

Can traffic school replace a lawyer?

Sometimes school helps on eligible non-criminal tickets—if you elect in time. It does not cover DUI, driving while suspended, many 30+ mph cases, or situations where dismissal/reduction is the better play. An attorney sorts which path fits your citation.

I got a Miami school-zone camera ticket—do I still need counsel?

Often yes. Camera volume in Miami-Dade is high; community reporting shows cameras dominate local speeding dockets. Deadlines still run. Call before you pay.
